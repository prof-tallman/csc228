# Responding to Fear Activity #

This activity gives students an opportunity to apply ideas from the opening chapters of *Beyond Fear* and the recent psychology and theology guest lectures to ordinary situations involving fear, worry, and uncertainty.

The purpose is not to ask students to disclose their own fears. Instead, students work in groups with fictional scenarios and consider what a wise response might look like. The activity should help students recognize that many of the same questions used to evaluate security decisions--danger, likelihood, uncertainty, costs, and tradeoffs--can also illuminate everyday worries.

## Materials ##

Prepare several short fictional scenarios involving fears that are recognizable to undergraduate students but unlikely to require personal disclosure. Possible examples include:

- A student who is afraid of failing an important class
- A senior who is worried about finding a job after graduation
- A student who dreads giving a presentation
- Someone who is worried about an unexpected financial expense
- A student who is afraid of making the wrong career decision
- Someone who is worried about a family member traveling
- A student who is anxious about making a significant mistake
- A student who is worried about disappointing a professor, coach, or mentor
- Someone who is anxious about moving to a new city or homesick
- A student who is afraid of being left out of an important social or professional opportunity
- Someone who is worried about making a bad first impression at an interview or internship
- Someone who is afraid of being judged after sharing an unpopular opinion
- A student who is anxious about taking on too many responsibilities and not being able to keep up
- Someone who is anxious about traveling alone for the first time
- A student who is afraid that new technology or AI will make their future career less secure

Prepare the scenarios on a set of popsicle sticks or another physical object that the students can draw from.

## Introduction ##

Begin by reminding students that the class has approached fear from several different directions. *Beyond Fear* has introduced ideas about danger, risk, perception, and the costs of security. The guest speakers have considered fear from psychological and theological perspectives. The goal of this activity is to bring those perspectives together.

It may be helpful to explicitly tell students that they will not be asked to share their own fears or personal experiences. They are welcome to make personal connections if they wish, but the activity is designed around fictional cases so that students can participate fully without revealing private information.

A useful opening question is:

> When someone is afraid, how can we tell whether a response is wise, excessive, or insufficient?

## Group Activity ##

Each group chooses a fictional scenario or draws one at random from the set. Ask the students first to understand the fear itself before trying to solve the problem. Possible discussion prompts include:

- What is the person actually afraid will happen?
- How serious would that outcome be?
- How likely does it seem to be?
- What uncertainty is involved?
- Is there any reason the person might be exaggerating or minimizing the danger?

Students should then consider possible responses:

- What reasonable actions could reduce the danger or help the person prepare for it?
- What would those actions cost in time, money, freedom, relationships, stress, or effort?
- Could an attempt to eliminate the danger completely become excessive?
- Could the response to the fear eventually become more harmful than the original danger?
- What might distinguish reasonable caution from a response driven primarily by fear?

At this point, ask the groups to reconsider the scenario through the three perspectives the class has encountered: psychology, theology, and security risk analysis. Keep in mind that sometimes the three perspectives will agree and reinforce each other, but there may also be significant differences between the disciplines.

### Psychology ###

What might the psychiatrist or psychologist want this person to understand about fear, worry, perception, or the body's response to danger?

### Theology ###

What might a Christian theological perspective contribute? Students might consider trust, courage, wisdom, responsibility, love of neighbor, and the limits of human control.

### Security and Risk ###

What ideas from the opening chapters of *Beyond Fear* help clarify the situation? Students do not yet need Schneier's complete framework. The emphasis should remain on danger, perceived danger, uncertainty, and the costs of attempts to become more secure.

## Class Sharing ##

Finally, ask each group to agree on a response to the larger question:

> What would it look like for the person in this scenario to respond to the fear wisely and honorably?

After a few minutes, bring the class back together and have each group briefly present its scenario and conclusions. Rather than having groups report every answer, ask them to summarize:

- The central fear in the scenario
- One important insight about the risk or perception of risk
- One response that seemed wise
- One response that might go too far
- One idea from psychology, theology, or *Beyond Fear* that influenced their thinking

As the groups present, look for recurring themes across otherwise different scenarios.

## Debrief ##

Conclude by returning to the larger purpose of the course. Security decisions are rarely about eliminating all danger. Everyday fears often involve the same problem: uncertainty remains, and attempts to gain complete security may carry significant costs of their own. If a parting question is needed, consider something like this:

> When we cannot eliminate danger or uncertainty, how should we decide what a wise response to fear looks like?
